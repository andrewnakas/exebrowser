Where to legally get classic Windows and DOS games in 2026

By Andrew Nakas · Published July 1, 2026 · ~7 minute read

There has never been a better time to play classic PC games — and never more confusion about where you're allowed to get them. Search for almost any 90s title and the top results are "abandonware" sites of, at best, uncertain legality. Here's the good news: a surprisingly large slice of the classic Windows and DOS catalog is genuinely, verifiably free — not because copyright expired (it almost never has), but because of how those games were licensed in the first place, or because their owners deliberately set them free later. This guide sorts the license-clean sources from the wishful thinking, category by category, with real examples you can play today.

Official freewareThe owner released it free. Tyrian 2000, One Must Fall 2097, SkiFree.

1. Shareware that was always meant to be copied

The biggest free-and-legal category exists because of a business model. In the early 90s, before online stores, publishers like Apogee and id Software sold games in episodes: the first episode was given away on purpose — copied between friends, uploaded to bulletin boards, stuffed onto magazine cover disks — and if you liked it, you mailed in payment for the rest. Redistribution of that first episode wasn't merely tolerated; it was the entire marketing plan. The license text that shipped in the archive typically spelled it out: copy this freely, keep the package unmodified, call it shareware (not "free" or "public domain"), and don't charge more than a small fee for the disk or the download itself.

Those licenses never expired, which is why the first episode of DOOM ("Knee-Deep in the Dead") and the first episode of Wolfenstein 3D are still freely and legally redistributable in 2026 — ExeBrowser hosts both, playable in one click. Jazz Jackrabbit's shareware episode is in the same family, though Epic's license attaches enough conditions (correct labeling, no alterations, credit to Epic) that we treat it as a bring-your-own-files guide rather than a hosted download. The critical limit to understand: shareware terms cover the shareware episode only. DOOM's episodes two and three were the commercial product, and they are exactly as copyrighted today as they were in 1993.

2. Games their owners later released as freeware

A second wave of legal free games arrived when rights holders decided an aging back catalog was worth more as goodwill than as revenue. Tyrian 2000, the beloved vertical-scrolling shooter, was released as freeware with its rights holders' blessing in the mid-2000s. Epic did the same for its fighting game One Must Fall 2097. And some classics were free from the start: SkiFree, the yeti-infested Windows skiing game, is distributed free by its author, Chris Pirih, from his own website. Adventure fans got Revolution Software's Beneath a Steel Sky as freeware in 2003, donated to the ScummVM project.

Two cautions. Freeware is not open source — you may download and play, but the code and assets remain proprietary, and the owner sets the terms. And the label is only as good as its paper trail: a real freeware release traces back to an announcement by the rights holder, not to a download site's say-so. Plenty of "freeware" tags on aggregator sites are wishful thinking. When we can't verify a title's status ourselves, we don't host it — and you should apply the same skepticism.

3. Open-source recreations and content-free engines

Some of the healthiest retro gaming today runs on code that is genuinely free software. id Software released the Doom engine's source in 1997 and relicensed it under the GPL in 1999, which spawned dozens of source ports — including Chocolate Doom, an engine so faithful it reproduces the original's bugs. But an engine is only half a game: Chocolate Doom ships with no levels, art, or music, and needs a WAD data file to run. You can supply the freely redistributable shareware DOOM1.WAD, buy the full game, or use Freedoom — a complete, from-scratch, freely licensed replacement for Doom's content, built by volunteers over two decades. Engine plus Freedoom equals a full game with zero proprietary bytes, which is why ExeBrowser can host it outright.

The same pattern shows up elsewhere. Freeciv is a GPL-licensed strategy game in the spirit of the Civilization series, developed openly since 1996. And Windows' most famous time-sink, 3D Pinball: Space Cadet, now exists as a community source-code reconstruction published openly on GitHub — the build ExeBrowser hosts is compiled from that open code, while Microsoft's original PINBALL.EXE remains proprietary and stays a bring-your-own-copy guide.

4. Storefronts that sell licensed classics

For everything still commercial, the boring answer is also the best one: buy it. GOG built its business on licensed, DRM-free re-releases of exactly these games, usually pre-configured with DOSBox or ScummVM so they run on a modern PC, and usually priced under ten dollars. Steam carries a large classic catalog too, and Zoom Platform specializes in licensed classics other stores skipped. A purchase has a useful side effect: it's the clean way to obtain data files for open-source engines. Buy Doom once on GOG and its WADs are yours to load into any source port you like.

5. Genuinely liberated and public-domain titles

The rarest category is games where the owner freed everything — code and data. Warzone 2100, the 1999 strategy game, is the textbook case: its source was GPL-licensed in 2004 and its remaining assets were freed afterward, so the community edition is fully libre. Beyond a handful of documented cases like that, treat "public domain" claims about 90s commercial games with heavy suspicion. Genuine public-domain dedications by rights holders are rare, deliberate, and written down. A download site's banner is not one.

The gray zones, honestly

"Abandonware" is not a legal category. It's a community label meaning "nobody seems to be selling this," and copyright law doesn't care. In the United States, a corporate work stays protected for 95 years from publication — a 1992 game is copyrighted into the 2080s. Rights to "dead" games routinely survive studio closures, passing to publishers, creditors, or IP-holding companies. Abandonware sites operate on forbearance: they host files and hope no takedown arrives. Often none does — as GOG's catalog proves, those rights frequently still have owners who notice.

The Internet Archive is complicated. It's a real preservation institution, and some of its software collections exist under specific agreements with rights holders or under preservation exemptions in copyright law. But its software library also contains a great deal of plainly unlicensed user-uploaded material sitting alongside the legitimate items. Judge it item by item; the fact that a file is on archive.org is not, by itself, a license to redistribute it.

Your own CD is your strongest card. If you bought a game, you own that copy, and the law has long recognized an owner's right to actually use the copy they own — including, in the U.S., making the copies essential to running it. Pulling the Pinball folder off your old Windows XP machine to play your own Space Cadet is a very different act from downloading a stranger's upload. This is precisely what ExeBrowser's bring-your-own-copy guides are built around.

A good-faith note: this article is general information about how classic games have historically been licensed and sold — it is not legal advice.
